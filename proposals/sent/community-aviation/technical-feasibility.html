Technical Feasibility Assessment

Rendered Flight Instruction

An engineering read of the Rendered Flight Instruction concept — what is certain, what must be verified before anyone commits, and where the concept carries dependencies it does not yet declare.

What this document is

An engineering read of the concept. It states three things and nothing else:

  1. What is technically certain — what can be built today, and with what.
  2. What must be verified before anyone commits — items that are cheap to resolve but currently unresolved, and which we will not assert until they are.
  3. Where the concept has undeclared dependencies — two claims in the current document rest on technical assumptions the document does not name.

This is not a proposal and contains no pricing. It does name every category of cost the project carries, so that none of them arrives later as a surprise. Numbers follow a scoping phase, not the other way around.

Source material reviewed: Rendered Flight Instruction concept document; Google Meet transcript, July 27 (83 min).

Verdict

The concept is buildable. The economics hold. It is engineering, not research.

With three qualifications:

  • The claim “rendered from real flight data” has a dependency the document does not declare. It is resolvable, but it must be resolved before the phrase is used publicly (§4.1).
  • The visual quality ceiling of the entire library is set by one asset — the aircraft model — and that is the single largest unquantified cost in the project (§4.2).
  • The AI finishing layer is not reliable today on the geometry that matters, and should be removed from the critical path. The business case survives intact without it (§4.3).

Everything else in the concept document is achievable with current, licensable, commercially clear technology.

What the system actually is

The concept document’s strongest line is “one artifact rendered three ways.” That instinct is correct, and it is the entire architecture. Stated precisely:

The deliverable is not video. The deliverable is a scenario file. Video is one of its renderers.

A scenario is a declarative, version-controlled file with separated layers:

scenario/
├── flight-track    aircraft state over time — position, attitude (pitch/roll/yaw),
│                   AoA, airspeed, configuration, control surface deflection
├── camera-script   named camera behaviours + timing ("chase-locked-stall")
├── overlay-track   which vectors / cones / labels are drawn, and when
├── environment     terrain (lat/lon), weather, time of day, light
├── narration       instructional beats, time-synced to the track
└── assessment      per-phase tolerances (drives future auto-grading)

Why this matters commercially, not just technically

  • It is the moat. A competitor can copy a look in a month. A library of CFI-validated scenario files is not copyable — it is accumulated authority, and it carries Rich Stowell’s signature.
  • It answers the open question about instructor authoring. Instructors submit flight profiles, not footage. They contribute pedagogy; the system guarantees house style. That is a marketplace with zero quality variance.
  • It decouples every layer. The render engine can be replaced in 2027 without touching the library. If the schema is designed first, nothing built on top of it is ever thrown away. If it is designed last, every video is a one-off craft object.

Six layers, each independently replaceable

[1] DATA       parsers (GPX / CSV / FDR / avionics logs) → normalizer → validated scenario
[2] GEOMETRY   track replay in a 3D engine over real terrain, scripted cameras → plates
[3] FINISH     optional AI restyle — see §4.3, currently out of scope
[4] OVERLAYS   deterministic vector pass, generated from the scenario file
[5] ASSEMBLY   grade / typography / disclosure / audio / encode
[6] DELIVERY   web player with switchable synchronized angles

Architectural rule — non-negotiable

Overlays are generated from the scenario data and composited over the finished plate. They are never produced or touched by a generative model. They are instructional data — a hallucinated wind vector is not an artifact, it is a credibility incident.

Critical technical findings

Where the geometry comes from — and what “real flight data” actually requires

This is the most important finding in this document.

A GPS track — from ForeFlight, a portable ADS-B receiver, or a consumer logger — records position, altitude, groundspeed and track. It does not record attitude: pitch, roll, yaw, angle of attack, or control surface deflection.

For cruise, pattern work or an approach, attitude can be approximated from the trajectory with acceptable accuracy. For a stall, a spin or an engine-out — precisely the priority maneuvers named in the concept document — attitude is the lesson. A spin produces a nearly vertical GPS track that contains no information about the rotation, which is the only thing worth teaching.

Compounding this: simulator flight models are weakest exactly in this part of the envelope. Post-stall behaviour and spin dynamics are approximations in every consumer simulator. Pilots detect this instantly, and this audience is the most unforgiving one that exists for this failure.

So the geometry for the priority maneuvers cannot come from a GPS track, and cannot come from default simulator physics either. There are three honest resolutions:

OptionWhat it requiresEffect on the claim
A — Instrumented captureAn aircraft with attitude logging (certain Garmin units log pitch/roll), or a dedicated AHRS/IMU on a capture flight“Rendered from real flight data” holds literally. Highest credibility
B — CFI-authored, physics-validatedManeuver flown in a high-fidelity model, reviewed and signed off by Rich or a CFI panelClaim becomes “physics-accurate, CFI-validated.” Still strong, but a different claim
C — HybridReal data where instrumentation exists; authored and validated where it does not, disclosed per scenarioMost practical. Requires per-scenario provenance tracking in the schema

Recommendation: C, with provenance recorded as a field in every scenario file, so the marketing claim is always exactly true for the specific clip.

Flag

The marketing line at the center of the concept document depends on a data source the document does not specify. This must be decided before the phrase is printed anywhere.

The aircraft model sets the quality ceiling of the entire library

Every shot in the product contains the same object. Its fidelity is the product’s fidelity.

Stock 3D aircraft models in the $200–500 range are game-quality: acceptable at distance, and they fall apart in exactly the shot that sells this project — a close pass on a wing, a propeller, a control surface moving through a deflection. The concept document’s entire value proposition is the impossible camera, which means the close camera.

A hero-quality model with correctly separated, animatable control surfaces, accurate proportions and film-grade materials is a commissioned asset with weeks of lead time and a cost in the four-to-five figure range. It is a one-time, fully amortized cost across the whole library — but it is real, it is large, and it is currently unquantified.

This is the single largest cost risk in the project. It is resolvable with a half-day of market survey and two or three quotes.

The AI finishing layer is not reliable on the geometry that matters

Video-to-video restyling maintains temporal consistency well on organic surfaces and poorly on thin, high-frequency, fast-moving geometry — propellers, control surface gaps, antennas, landing gear. These are precisely the details a pilot audience reads first.

Recommendation: remove the AI layer from the critical path entirely. Treat it as an upside to be demonstrated later, never as a dependency.

Two reasons this costs nothing:

  1. The business case does not need it. The value proposition is impossible camera angles, overlays on real geometry, and marginal cost per variant approaching zero. All three are properties of the rendering pipeline, not of the AI layer.
  2. A clean, well-lit render already carries the shot. Lighting, materials and grade deliver most of the perceived quality. AI is finishing — and the concept document already says so.

The “openly AI” positioning remains correct and strategically valuable — the pipeline is AI-assisted in authoring, scenario generation and instruction design. It simply should not be load-bearing for on-screen geometry.

Terrain: the best-looking option may not be the licensable one

Photorealistic global 3D terrain is what makes the mountain, canyon and coastal scenarios possible — a genuine pedagogical unlock that no flight school can demonstrate. The most photorealistic source carries usage terms whose applicability to commercially distributed rendered video needs to be read before it is designed around.

A fallback exists (standard world terrain plus licensed imagery) and is legally straightforward. It is less spectacular, but it is fully sufficient for the instructional beat — where the concept document itself correctly calls for a flat, legible frame rather than a dramatic one.

Flag

Decide this before look development begins, not after. It sets the visual target.

What a prototype should be, technically

The demo is not one beautiful clip. It is one maneuver rendered many ways from one file — because that is the business model, made visible.

A single polished clip proves you can make a video. One maneuver appearing in four synchronized angles, a night variant and a weather variant — all traceably generated from the same source file — proves you have built a machine. The first is a commercial. The second is a company. Investors buy the second.

Recommended prototype scope — one maneuver, frozen

ManeuverPower-off stall. Highest instructional demand, legible geometry, and the “what the pilot sees vs. what is actually happening” contrast is strongest at the break. Lower data risk than a spin

OverlaysRelative wind, angle of attack, lift/load vector, airspeed & altitude ribbon, maneuver phase markers

ValidationReview pass by Rich and a CFI panel before anything is shown externally

Explicitly excluded from a prototype

Stated so that nobody assumes otherwise.

  • AI video finishing layer (§4.3)
  • Full photorealistic production pipeline
  • Commissioned hero aircraft model
  • More than one maneuver
  • Instructor authoring tool
  • Course site, LMS, scheduling, flight ops, mobile app
  • Curriculum content authoring
  • Any regulatory or FAA credit claim
  • Live-action footage (cockpit, walkaround, preflight)

The last exclusion is deliberate and matches the concept document: synthetic imagery does not serve preflight, control feel, or the credibility beat where a student needs to see that a real aircraft and a real instructor exist. The honest hybrid — real once per topic, rendered for geometry and maneuvers — is a strength of the concept, and should be stated as such rather than hidden.

Red flags

Raised before anything else, as they should be.

  1. Credibility is one clip deep. A single published video with implausible physics, in front of the most unforgiving technical audience that exists, is not recoverable by later quality. Every maneuver requires CFI sign-off before publication, without exception. This gate is not negotiable and should be treated as a release blocker, not a review step.
  2. The central marketing claim has an undeclared dependency (§4.1). Resolve before printing.
  3. One asset caps the quality of everything (§4.2), and it is currently unpriced.
  4. The AI layer is the most likely source of a broken promise (§4.3). Removing it from the critical path removes the risk at no cost to the business case.
  5. Terrain licensing may constrain the visual target (§4.4). Decide before look development.
  6. Nothing may be hand-finished outside the pipeline. A one-off manual retouch on a single clip breaks both the consistency promise and the re-render economics that the entire cost argument depends on. If it does not go through the pipeline, it does not ship.
  7. Prototype scope must be frozen in writing. A fundraising push generates continuous pressure toward “can we also add…”. Each addition compounds against a fixed demo date.
  8. No regulatory claims. Free ground school does not require FAA training credit, and claiming it without basis is unnecessary legal exposure.
  9. The concept document is strategy, not verified engineering. Its reasoning is sound and its economics hold, but none of its technical claims had been validated before this assessment. It should be treated as direction, not as specification.

Recommended path

Phase 0 · approximately one week

Technical spike

The only honest way to deliver cost certainty is to buy the certainty cheaply first. A short, bounded spike resolving every item in §5 and producing:

  • A written licensing verdict — engine, terrain, frameworks
  • Real quotes for the hero aircraft model
  • A resolution for the attitude-data question, with Rich and Billy’s input
  • A measured render benchmark at target quality
  • A 10-second test render — the look test that settles the visual question
  • Scenario schema v0

Prototype

Scoped per §6, with a frozen scope and a fixed date. Not scheduled or estimated until Phase 0 closes; any timeline offered before that would be a guess presented as a commitment.

Summary

The concept is sound and the architecture is correct. The economic argument — fixed cost replacing variable cost, marginal cost per angle and per variant approaching zero, one artifact serving video, simulator and assessment — holds under engineering scrutiny.

What the concept document does not yet contain is the set of dependencies that determine whether it can be said out loud: where trustworthy attitude data comes from, what the aircraft asset costs, which terrain is licensable, and which claims survive contact with a pilot audience.

Those are answerable in about a week. We would rather answer them before anyone commits to a number than discover them together afterwards.
